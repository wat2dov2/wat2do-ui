Wat2Do promotes an event (a carousel slide, the events feed's featured row) only when it passes both tests: any student at the school can go, and a typical student there would want to (Tony, 2026-11-25: "the point of these carousels is to promote events anyone can AND would want to attend").

**1. Open to all: any student at the school can attend.**
These fail it:
- events for one residence, floor or house, or for a set of roommates or trip-mates, such as a ski club's roomie mixer for the people already going on its trip;
- members-only, exec-only, team-only, invite-only or private events, and anything a club says is reserved for its own students;
- events for one program, faculty, department, year, cohort or class, such as a fourth-year nursing formal, a biology students' party, an engineering semi-formal, a medical students' fashion show or a portfolio class's screening;
- events for graduate students only, or for the students of one professional school (law, medicine, business, dentistry) only;
- events for one identity group, such as one ethnicity or nationality's own members (an African students' night, a Punjabi students' social), one religion, one gender or one sexuality.
These pass it:
- an event open to everyone where members get a discount or first pick of tickets;
- an event framed for first-years or newcomers that welcomes everyone;
- a ticketed event any student can buy a ticket to;
- a cultural event put on for every student to enjoy, such as a Chinese night market, a Lunar New Year fair, a multicultural food festival or a K-pop dance night;
- a faculty society's or identity club's event whose post invites every student ("all programs and majors welcome", "everyone is welcome").
When a post speaks to one group ("hey nursing!", "calling all Agros", "meet fellow caregivers") and does not say others are welcome, treat the event as for that group only.

**2. Broad appeal: a typical student at the school would want to go for fun, whatever they study.**
Good fits: parties, socials and mixers open to all, free food, concerts, shows, sports games, games nights and tournaments, festivals, markets, trips, hands-on making, new activities to try and big free giveaways.
These fail it even when anyone may attend:
- pre-med, pre-health, pre-dental and pre-pharmacy events: medical school panels and info sessions, MCAT prep, premed talks, suturing, injection or other clinical-skills workshops;
- every law or legal-career event: pre-law and law society panels, law school info sessions and tours, mooting, mock trials, legal networking and LSAT prep (and a law school's own student societies hold their events for law students, which fails the first test);
- other career-track and one-industry events: info sessions, recruiting and career panels, networking nights, firm or company nights, career fairs, resume reviews, headshot sessions and case competitions;
- academic events: research talks, lab tours, journal clubs, webinars, study sessions, tutoring, exam reviews, test prep (GRE, GMAT) and program info nights;
- a club's general meetings, even when open to everyone: GMs, first meetings, AGMs, info and intro meetings, elections and town halls.
Two kinds pass it despite that list: a competition any student can enter for fun with a big real prize (a cash prize or prize pool in the hundreds of dollars or more, a trip, real tech), and an in-person appearance by a household-name company or person most students already know.
A club in one of those fields can still throw an event for everyone, judged on its own: a pre-dental club's trivia party or a pre-law society's holiday social open to every student passes both tests.
